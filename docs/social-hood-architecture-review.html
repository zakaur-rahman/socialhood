Architecture review

How Social Hood should handle webhooks, analytics and AI at scale

A review of what Social Hood actually has to process, what the platforms and vendors allow today, and the smallest architecture that handles it reliably, with the measurable points at which each larger piece becomes worth adding.

Scope webhooks, queues, storage, analytics, AI, multi-tenancyEvidence date 28 Sep 2026Audience owner and engineers building v2
Verified read on an official page (see Sources) Recommendation this review's design choice Assumption an input to the model, not a fact Estimate derived from assumptions and verified prices Unknown needs testing with a real account

In one paragraph

PostgreSQL alone is enough for the data, and enough for the queue at MVP scale. Kafka, ClickHouse, a separate vector database and Kubernetes are not needed now. The workload in your brief (100,000 comments and 50,000 DMs a day, a 100,000-comment viral spike) averages a few events per second with peaks in the low hundreds, which one PostgreSQL instance absorbs comfortably. What actually limits Social Hood is not infrastructure but the platforms: Instagram lets an account send only 750 automated private replies an hour, insights can lag 48 hours, webhooks must be answered within 5 seconds or the app gets unsubscribed, and WhatsApp onboarding is capped at 10 new businesses a week until you are verified. The architecture below is built around those limits. AI cost, not compute, is the largest variable expense, at roughly $0.20–0.25 per connected account per month in the brief's scenario.

1Final decision for the MVP

Database
PostgreSQL 18 on Render (start at 1 CPU / 4 GB, $55/month plus storage) with pgvector, pg_trgm, pgcrypto, pg_stat_statements. The only system of record. Recommendation
Cache
Valkey (Render Key Value, 256 MB, $10/month), used only for real-time event streams to browsers, rate-limit counters, short caches and locks. Nothing in it needs to survive a restart. Recommendation
Queue
A PostgreSQL-backed job queue (Procrastinate), replacing ARQ, which its maintainers put in maintenance-only mode. Jobs carry ids; the domain tables hold the state. Two lanes: interactive and bulk. Recommendation
Workers
One Render background worker running two processes (interactive lane, bulk lane); add instances horizontally. Recommendation
Analytics
PostgreSQL tables: one wide row per post per snapshot window, one row per account per day, rollups computed on write. No separate analytics database. Recommendation
Vector DB
No dedicated vector database. pgvector in the same PostgreSQL, used only for business knowledge (and later post captions). Recommendation
RAG
Yes, but narrow: only for replies that need business facts (policies, shipping, products). Greetings, analytics and order questions use no retrieval, SQL or tools. Recommendation
LLM
Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite for classification and most replies; Gemini 3.8 Flash only for complex replies and long summaries; Gemini Embedding 2 at 768 dimensions. Gemini 2.5 models are closed to new projects. Paid tier only. Recommendation
Object storage
Cloudinary for media (publishing needs public URLs; inbound media must be copied because platform URLs expire). Cloudflare R2 later for archives and exports. Recommendation
Monitoring
Sentry (errors, performance, cron monitors) plus Render metrics and logs; alerts on webhook failures, queue lag and AI spend. Recommendation
Webhook strategy
Verify → split → store once → enqueue → 200, in under 500 ms. Never fail a webhook for a processing error. Reconciliation polling fills gaps; a daily job re-checks every account's webhook subscription. Recommendation
AI architecture
A fixed pipeline, not a free-roaming agent: rules pre-filter → cheap classification (batched for comments) → retrieval router → reply generation → 13-check safety policy → send. Tool-calling agents (Ask Social Hood, operator assistant) come later with read-only tools first. Recommendation

Estimated monthly infrastructure at launch: about $200 (Render workspace, API, worker, PostgreSQL, Valkey, Vercel Pro, Sentry Team), plus Cloudinary once past its free tier, plus AI usage. Estimate The decision matrix gives the reason for every component, including those left out.

3Webhook gateway

Answer: yes, validate → persist → return 200 → process asynchronously, with two refinements: split each POST into its individual events before storing, and treat the database write as both the durable log and the duplicate check. Recommendation

Why this shape

  • Meta sets a hard deadline. Messaging webhooks must be answered "within 5 or less seconds". If delivery keeps failing for 1 hour, Meta sends a "Webhooks Disabled" alert and unsubscribes the app from that Instagram account. Verified [S3] Any AI call, platform call or slow query inside the request puts every tenant's subscription at risk. v1 ran up to 15 s of AI before answering.
  • Meta retries and duplicates. Instagram: "retry immediately, then … over the next 36 hours … Your server should handle deduplication." WhatsApp: retries "for up to 7 days". Verified [S2][S8] A 200 must mean "stored durably", and storing must be idempotent.
  • No replay exists. "You will not be able to query historical webhook event notification data." Verified [S1] If Social Hood drops an event, it is gone; only reconciliation polling can recover part of it.
  • Batches mix objects. One POST can carry up to 1,000 updates, from different accounts of the same type. Verified [S1] Splitting lets each event be routed to its tenant, retried and deduplicated independently.

Missing, delayed and out-of-order events

  • Delayed or out of order: order everything by the platform's own timestamp (messaging[].timestamp, statuses[].timestamp), never by arrival time. The envelope's time is when the notification was sent, not when the event happened. Verified [S1] Status changes only move forward (sent → delivered → read); a late "delivered" after "read" is ignored.
  • Missing comments: for posts under 7 days old, every 30 minutes compare the post's comments_count with Social Hood's stored count, and fetch comments only when they differ. That costs one cheap call per post most of the time. Recommendation Note: the comments endpoint returns top-level comments only, 50 per query, with no timestamp filter. Verified [S7i]
  • Missing DMs: the Conversations API only exposes the 20 most recent messages per thread and allows 2 calls per second per account. Verified [S7i] Use it only for accounts whose webhook traffic stopped unexpectedly (no events for 6 h during their usual active hours), not as a routine sync.
  • Subscription loss: the daily re-subscribe above, plus an alert when a normally busy account goes silent.

Lanes, backpressure and fairness

  • Two lanes. interactive: webhook processing, inbound DM analysis, suggestions, sends, scheduled sends. bulk: comment analysis, backfills, media sync, metric snapshots, summaries, knowledge ingestion. Each lane has its own worker processes, so a viral post never delays a customer's DM reply. Recommendation
  • Per-tenant caps in the bulk lane. At most 4 concurrent bulk jobs per workspace (a Valkey semaphore). One tenant's 100,000 comments then use 4 slots while everyone else's work continues. Recommendation
  • Per-account platform token buckets. Instagram Send API 100/s for text and 10/s for audio or video, private replies 750/hour, Conversations API 2/s; WhatsApp 80 messages/s per number and 1 message every 6 s to the same user. Verified [S5][S9] Jobs that would exceed a bucket are re-scheduled for when a token frees, not retried blindly.
  • Backpressure signal. Queue depth and the age of the oldest ready job per lane are exported as metrics; alert when the interactive lane's oldest job is older than 30 s.
  • Dead letters. A job that exhausts its retries stays in the queue table as failed, with its error; its domain row is marked failed with a reason. An admin view lists them; a "retry" action re-enqueues.

6PostgreSQL as the primary database

Answer: yes. Every entity in the brief (users, organisations, teams, accounts, posts, comments, conversations, messages, automations, agent configuration, leads, AI outputs, analytics metadata, AI logs) is relational, tenant-keyed and queried by index. PostgreSQL also covers full-text search (tsvector), fuzzy matching (pg_trgm), vectors (pgvector) and partitioning, all available on Render. Verified [S21] Recommendation

Design rules

  • Indexes follow the access patterns above, always leading with the tenant or parent key: (workspace_id, status, last_message_at DESC, id DESC) for the inbox, partial indexes for small filters (WHERE awaiting_reply), GIN for full-text, HNSW for vectors. No index without a query that needs it.
  • Partitioning, later: range-partition messages, comments and webhook_events by month once a trigger in §19 fires. Dropping a partition "is far faster than a bulk operation", and "unique and primary keys must include the partition key". Verified [S31] The inbox's keyset queries already carry a time bound, so pruning works. pg_partman is available on Render, but without its background worker, so maintenance runs from a Social Hood cron job. Verified [S21] webhook_events is the first candidate: its 30-day purge becomes dropping a daily or weekly partition.
  • Connection pooling: Render's built-in PgBouncer runs in transaction mode. Verified [S21] The API can use it once connection counts grow; asyncpg then needs prepared-statement caching disabled (statement_cache_size=0). Workers that LISTEN for jobs connect directly. Connection limit is 100 below 8 GB RAM, 200 at 8–16 GB. Verified [S21]
  • Read replicas, later: for analytics and reporting reads once they measurably slow the primary (§19). Render supports up to 5, each billed like the primary. Verified [S21]
  • Retention and archiving: the purge job enforces plan retention (Free: 90 days of messages), 30 days of raw webhook payloads, 13 months of AI usage events. Archive to object storage (R2, JSON lines) only when a customer or regulation needs it.
  • Backups: point-in-time recovery, 7 days on Render Pro workspaces. Verified [S21] Rehearse a restore before launch.

Where PostgreSQL stops being enough

Not at hundreds of millions of rows as such: indexed point and range queries stay fast on well-partitioned tables. It becomes the wrong tool for interactive aggregations that scan tens of millions of rows (for example "engagement across all posts of all accounts by week for the last year", or a cross-tenant benchmark), and for very high write rates of append-only events (thousands per second, sustained). Both have concrete triggers in §19. Neither is in the R1 or R2 feature set. Recommendation

Why pgvector and not Qdrant, Pinecone, Weaviate or Milvus: the vectors belong to rows that already live in PostgreSQL, must be filtered by workspace, and must disappear when a source is edited or a workspace is deleted. Keeping them in the same database makes that a transaction instead of a sync job. pgvector 0.8.6 supports HNSW indexes up to 2,000 dimensions (4,000 with halfvec) and iterative index scans, which fix the problem of a tenant filter starving results. When to reconsider: more than about 20 million vectors, retrieval p95 above 150 ms after tuning, or the vector index no longer fitting in RAM. Then first try halfvec and list-partitioning by workspace (the pgvector README's suggestion for multi-tenancy), and only then a dedicated service. Verified [S25] Recommendation

12Agent architecture

Two different "agents" hide in the brief, and they should be built differently. Recommendation

  • The customer-facing reply agent (R1) should be a fixed pipeline, not an open tool-calling loop: classify → route → retrieve → draft → check → send. Every step is predictable, testable and cheap, and it can never decide to take an action nobody reviewed.
  • The owner-facing assistant (Ask Social Hood, automation builder; R3) is where tool calling pays off: open-ended questions, several steps, a human watching. It starts with read-only tools; action tools always go through an approval card (the pattern already prototyped in v1's agent code).

Pipeline rules

  1. Rules before models. Skip analysis for messages that are only emoji, stickers, reactions, "ok", "thanks" or tags; classify obvious keywords ("price", "link", "COD") with rules as hints.
  2. Debounce bursts. One analysis per burst of messages, not per message (4-second deferral keyed by conversation).
  3. Batch what is not urgent. Comments in batches of 50. Backfills and re-analysis go through the Batch API at half price; its turnaround target is 24 hours, so never for live traffic. Verified [S20]
  4. Small model first. Flash-Lite for classification and simple replies; 3.8 Flash only when the router flags complexity (thread longer than 20 messages, two or more questions, or a Flash-Lite draft that fails the confidence check).
  5. Cache by content. Identical comment text on the same post ("😍", "link please") reuses its classification. Viral posts repeat a lot. Recommendation
  6. Everything asynchronous, off the request path, with per-task timeouts (8 s analysis, 12 s reply) and one retry on 429 or 5xx.
  7. Fallbacks: if the reply model fails, fall back to Flash-Lite; if AI is unavailable altogether, the inbox works without suggestions, and Auto mode stops sending (it never sends a canned "technical difficulties" reply, as v1 did).
  8. Spend guard. Gemini's Tier 1 caps spend at $10 per rolling 10 minutes, with $250 total; Tier 2 needs $100 paid and 3 days. Verified [S20] Reach Tier 2 before launch, and add Social Hood's own global limit so a bug cannot spend the monthly budget in an afternoon.
  9. Paid tier only. Free-tier inputs are "used to … improve, and develop Google products", and humans may read them; paid-tier prompts are not. Verified [S20]

16Multi-tenancy

The hierarchy is Workspace (organisation) → members → connected accounts → posts, comments, conversations, messages → AI outputs. Every row below the workspace carries workspace_id.

ConcernDesign
IsolationThree layers from day one (route membership check; automatic ORM filter; an isolation test over every route), plus PostgreSQL row-level security as a fourth layer in R2. v1 leaked platform tokens across accounts because this was left to each endpoint.
Indexes and queriesEvery index on a tenant table starts with workspace_id or a parent key that implies it; list queries are keyset-paginated within one workspace; no query scans across tenants except internal analytics on a replica or warehouse.
RetentionPer plan (Free 90 days of messages), per platform rule (LinkedIn one year for reporting data), per request (workspace deletion within 24 h).
API rate limitsPer user and per workspace sliding windows in Valkey; heavier limits on AI endpoints.
Platform limitsToken buckets per connected account (they are per account at Meta), so one tenant exhausting its Instagram budget cannot slow another's.
Queue isolationInteractive and bulk lanes; at most 4 concurrent bulk jobs per workspace; optionally a third lane for Enterprise tenants later.
AI budgetsMonthly credit counters reserved atomically before each call; per-workspace alerts at 80% and 100%; a global spend guard above them.
Noisy neighbours in storageAt Growth scale, partition the largest tables by time (not tenant); if one tenant dominates, list-partition that tenant's vectors or move them to their own index.
